SoC, Board & Memory Map

Abstract. The previous chapter described the wall; this one describes the building it stands in. Redoubt is not an abstract design, it is a specific processor, built out of specific open-source pieces, running on a specific circuit board you can buy for about the price of a nice dinner. This chapter walks through that board and why it was chosen, the configurable RISC-V core we assemble on it and the exact plugins we switch on, the collection of peripherals wired around the core, the full physical memory map that ties the addresses of Chapter 3 to real hardware, and the unglamorous but load-bearing matters of clocking and reset. It closes with the network, screen, and randomness peripherals that make the device do something visible, the alternatives we turned down, and the questions still open. It assumes you have read Chapter 3 but nothing more.

Note to first-time readers.

A handful of hardware words recur below. Here they are in plain language.

FPGA
A field-programmable gate array: a chip full of blank logic that you configure into whatever digital circuit you like by loading a bitstream. We use one to build a RISC-V computer that does not otherwise exist as a physical part, which means the whole processor is something we, and you, can read and modify.
Soft core
A processor implemented as an FPGA design rather than etched into fixed silicon. Ours is VexRiscv. Because it is "soft," we choose exactly which features it has.
SoC
System-on-chip: the processor plus its memory controller and peripherals, all assembled into one design. We generate ours with a tool called LiteX so we do not hand-wire the tedious parts.
Peripheral
Anything attached to the processor that is not the processor or plain memory: a serial port, an SD-card controller, a screen driver. Software talks to a peripheral by reading and writing special addresses (see MMIO).
MMIO
Memory-mapped I/O: the trick of giving a hardware device an address, so that a store to that address pokes the device rather than storing to memory. It is why "the network hardware" can appear as a region in a memory map.
Bus
The shared set of wires over which the processor reaches memory and peripherals. Ours is a Wishbone/CSR bus; every peripheral hangs off it at some address.

4.1The board, and why this one

Redoubt runs on the ULX3S, a small open-hardware development board built around a Lattice ECP5-85F FPGA.1 Choosing hardware for a security project is mostly an exercise in saying no to things, and the ULX3S survived a short, strict list of requirements.

The first and least negotiable was a fully open toolchain. It would be faintly absurd to argue that a device is trustworthy because you can read all of its source, and then compile that source with a closed, unauditable vendor tool. The ECP5 is the sweet spot here: it is the largest FPGA family that the open toolchain, Yosys for synthesis, nextpnr for place-and-route, Project Trellis for the bitstream, supports well.2 Every step from our HDL to the bits that configure the chip runs on software we can inspect, and the device is flashed with an open loader (openFPGALoader). Nothing in the path asks us to trust a binary we cannot see into.

The second was enough room. The "85F" denotes roughly 85,000 lookup tables of logic, which is comfortable headroom for a VexRiscv core, the LiteX SoC around it, and the Monitor's needs, without the constant fitting battles a smaller part would force. The third was the right peripherals on the board already: 32 MB of SDRAM, a USB-serial bridge for the host link, an on-board ESP32 for network egress, a microSD slot for storage, QSPI flash for non-volatile state, eleven LEDs, and a footprint for a small SPI OLED. That inventory maps almost one-to-one onto what Redoubt needs, which means less adapter hardware and fewer things to get wrong. It is also, frankly, a board with a large and friendly tutorial base, and for a project meant to be built and hacked on rather than admired from a distance, that counts.3

There is a deliberate lineage here. Precursor, the open secure-communications device, runs the Xous Rust microkernel on a VexRiscv soft core generated by LiteX, on an FPGA. Redoubt reuses that exact stack (VexRiscv, LiteX, an FPGA, a small Rust kernel) and aims it at a different job. Walking a proven path leaves us free to spend our novelty budget on the one thing that is new: mediating an agent's tool calls.

4.2The processor: VexRiscv, and the switches we flip

VexRiscv is a RISC-V core written in SpinalHDL, and its defining trait is that it is assembled from plugins.4 You do not get a fixed processor; you get a kit, and you choose which capabilities to include. That is unusually valuable for us, because the security argument of Chapter 3 depends on the core actually having certain features, three privilege modes, real PMP, and lets us leave off anything we do not need, keeping the design small. The target instruction set is RV32IMAC: 32-bit base, integer multiply/divide, atomics, and the compressed encoding.

Here is the configuration, plugin by plugin, with why each is present.

CsrPlugin (M/S/U)
Provides the control-and-status registers and, crucially, all three privilege modes. Without this there is no machine/supervisor/user distinction, and the whole isolation scheme of Chapter 3 has nothing to stand on. This is the non-negotiable plugin.
PmpPlugin
Implements Physical Memory Protection, the load-bearing wall itself. We request at least eight entries, which covers the region map with a little room to spare, and we verify the implemented count at boot rather than assuming it. Everything in the last chapter is enforced here.
IBusCachedPlugin / DBusCachedPlugin
Instruction and data caches. These are about speed, not security, but they earn their place: fetching every instruction and datum straight from SDRAM would make the device sluggish. The caches sit beneath the PMP check, so they change performance, not what is permitted.
MulDivPlugin
Hardware multiply and divide (the M in RV32IMAC). The Monitor's hashing and the SoC's housekeeping want multiplication often enough that doing it in software would be a needless drag.
MmuPlugin (Sv32), optional
The full virtual-memory unit, giving the Warden page-based isolation between user-mode compartments. It is genuinely useful one level down, and it is explicitly optional for v1: PMP is the wall that must be there, and the MMU is a finer mesh we may add on top once the base is solid. Whether it ships in v1 is an open question (§4.8), carried jointly with Chapter 3.
ExternalInterruptPlugin
Routes interrupts from peripherals, a timer expiring, a device signalling, into the core so the Warden can schedule against real events.

The important thing to notice is how little of this is exotic. It is a plain, cache-equipped RV32IMAC core with the two features the design truly requires, three modes and PMP, turned on, and one heavier feature (the MMU) held in reserve. A small core is a core we can reason about.

4.3The SoC around the core

A bare processor is not much use; it needs memory and a way to reach the world. We assemble that with LiteX, a generator that stitches a core together with memory controllers and peripherals and hands back a working SoC.5 LiteX is, in its own words, boring on purpose, and that is precisely the recommendation for scaffolding that must simply work so the interesting part, the Monitor, can be built on top. Everything hangs off a Wishbone/CSR bus; each peripheral occupies a range of addresses, and reading or writing those addresses is how software drives it.

The peripherals we instantiate are these:

  • LiteDRAM, the controller for the 32 MB of SDRAM, which holds the Warden's working memory and the backing store for the user-mode compartments.
  • UART, the serial link. One instance carries the host connection over the on-board USB-serial bridge; it is how the agent's requests arrive and answers depart (the wire format is Chapter 6). A second serves as a debug console during development.
  • LiteSDCard, the microSD controller, Redoubt's storage egress. Its registers live in the machine-only egress region, so only the Monitor drives it.
  • QSPI flash, non-volatile storage for the bitstream, the Monitor image, and an M-only area holding secrets and keys loaded at boot.
  • GPIO / LEDs, the eleven on-board LEDs, invaluable for a live demo: a hijacked agent's request being denied can light up in the room.
  • SPI → OLED, an SPI link to a small SSD1331 display, which shows verdicts and the audit log's head hash where a person can read them.
  • UART/SPI → ESP32, the machine-owned link to the on-board ESP32, Redoubt's network egress.
  • RO-TRNG, a ring-oscillator true-random-number generator built in the FPGA fabric, feeding key and handle randomisation, with health tests described in §4.7.

Notice which of these the Monitor keeps for itself. The SD controller, the ESP32 link, and the TRNG are the wires and the entropy, the things that must not be reachable by untrusted code, and they are gathered into a single machine-only region so that Chapter 3's wall covers them all at once. The UART for the host, by contrast, is deliberately reachable by the user-mode endpoint, because carrying bytes in and out is all it does; it decides nothing.

4.4The physical memory map

Everything above meets here. The bus assigns each region of memory and each peripheral a range of physical addresses, and those ranges are the very ones Chapter 3's PMP entries police. This is the canonical map for the whole project; every other chapter refers to these addresses and none of them change.

Two rows deserve a second glance because they carry the security weight. The 0x1000_0000 block is the Monitor's world, its code, its data, and the secrets, and it is machine-only, invisible to everything below. The 0xF000_0000 block is not memory at all; it is the control registers of the network, storage, and randomness hardware, mapped into the address space so that "operate the network" is a matter of storing to an address. Because that block is machine-only, only the Monitor can issue those stores. Everything the untrusted world is allowed to see, the Warden's arena at 0x2000_0000, the compartments at 0x3000_0000, and the single shared mailbox at 0x4000_0000, is by construction devoid of anything worth stealing.

A subtlety worth flagging: WARDEN and COMPARTMENTS are address windows that the SoC backs with the physical SDRAM sitting at 0x8000_0000. The distinct low addresses give each tier a clean base for its PMP entry; the bytes themselves live in the one 32 MB SDRAM. Keeping the map's regions at tidy, well-separated bases is what lets the PMP table of Chapter 3 stay short and legible.

4.5Clocking and reset

Two pieces of plumbing rarely get the attention they deserve, and both bear on security more than they first appear. The first is the clock. The ECP5 has a phase-locked loop that multiplies the board's input oscillator up to the core's operating frequency and derives the separate clock the SDRAM controller needs. LiteX generates this clocking network; our part is to pick a frequency the fitted design actually meets timing at, which the place-and-route tools report. If the design does not meet timing it is not merely slow, it can be subtly wrong, so "the build passes timing" is a correctness gate, not a nicety.

The second is reset, and reset is where the whole trust story begins. When the ECP5 powers up it loads its bitstream from QSPI flash and releases the core from reset into machine mode, executing the boot ROM at 0x0000_0000. There is a precise, security-critical ordering that follows, the ROM measures the Monitor, the Monitor raises and locks the PMP wall before any lesser code runs, and Chapter 5 tells that story in full. What matters here is that the hardware guarantees the starting conditions: one core, in the highest privilege mode, running trusted ROM, with no untrusted software anywhere in the machine. Every guarantee in Chapter 3 is built on that clean start.

4.6The peripherals that make it real

Three peripherals turn Redoubt from a memory-protection demonstration into a device that does something. They are treated at length in Chapter 12, but they belong in the picture here.

ESP32 — the network egress

The on-board ESP32 provides Wi-Fi, and it is Redoubt's route to the network. The Monitor drives it over a machine-owned UART/SPI link inside EGRESS_MMIO; a user-mode network driver may format a payload in its own compartment, but the actual send is a Monitor action, gated by a capability check and with secrets injected at that moment. This is the arrangement that lets a fully compromised host reach the network only on the Monitor's terms.

It also comes with the project's most important honest caveat, and we state it plainly rather than bury it. The ESP32 runs its own, largely closed firmware, so that firmware sits in the egress path. The agent still cannot reach the network except through Redoubt, but a purist would want Redoubt to own the wire all the way down. The planned answer is a v2 board with wired Ethernet (an ECPIX-5 or Colorlight), where the Monitor owns the MAC directly. For a v1 demonstrator, the ESP32 is an acceptable, clearly-labelled compromise, not a hidden one.6

OLED and LEDs — the device speaking to a person

A reference monitor that silently does the right thing is hard to trust and harder to demonstrate. The eleven LEDs and the small SSD1331 OLED let Redoubt say what it just did: a denial can be shown as a code on the screen, the audit log's head hash can be displayed for an outside verifier to check, and an attack being contained can be visible in the room. This is genuinely part of the design's value, not decoration, a security property you can watch being enforced is one people believe.

TRNG — entropy you can trust

Randomness underlies key generation and the randomised capability handles that stop the host from guessing its way to authority. Ours is a ring-oscillator entropy source built directly in the FPGA fabric. Raw ring-oscillator output is not uniform, so it is conditioned, and, more importantly, it is watched: continuous health tests per NIST SP 800-90B (the Repetition-Count and Adaptive-Proportion tests) run on the raw stream, and if the source degrades, operations that consume entropy block rather than proceed on weak randomness.7 A silently failing RNG is one of the classic ways secure systems rot, so its failure here is designed to be loud.

4.7Resource and failure notes

A few practical realities of building this on a real, finite FPGA:

  • Fitting. The core, the caches, LiteDRAM, and the peripherals must all fit within the 85F's logic and its block RAM. The Monitor's tables and the machine-only regions compete for block RAM with the caches; the exact sizes of MON_DATA and SECRETS are therefore not final until the design is placed and routed. This is the same open item flagged in Chapter 3.
  • Timing. As above, a design that fails timing is treated as a failed build. The clock frequency is chosen to what the fit actually sustains, not to what we would like.
  • A slow or stuck peripheral. The egress devices are far slower than the core and can, in principle, hang. The Monitor addresses them against a timeout and returns an error rather than waiting forever (the mechanism is Chapter 9); a stuck ESP32 must not be able to freeze the guard.
  • PMP entry count. The whole isolation argument assumes the fitted PmpPlugin really implements the entries we asked for. We request at least eight and check the count at boot; a core that reports fewer fails to start rather than running with a hole in the wall.

4.8Alternatives rejected

The board and SoC could have been assembled differently. Here is what we declined, and why.

A bigger board with a closed toolchain (Arty A7-100T / Vivado). The Xilinx-based Arty offers more logic headroom, but only through Vivado, a large closed toolchain. For a device whose entire pitch is auditability, compiling the design with an unauditable tool undercuts the claim at the root. The open ECP5 flow was worth more than the extra LUTs.

A hardened commodity SoC instead of a soft core. An off-the-shelf microcontroller would be faster and cheaper to bring up, but we could neither read nor modify its processor, and much of the interest, and the assurance, of Redoubt is that the CPU itself is open and malleable. A soft core on an FPGA is slower and more work; it is also the point.

Wired Ethernet in v1. Owning the network wire directly (no ESP32 in the path) is the right end state and is planned for v2. Requiring it in v1 would have forced a different, less friendly board and delayed a working demonstrator for a purity gain that does not change the v1 guarantee against the untrusted host. We took the honest caveat and shipped.

Turning the MMU on unconditionally. Enabling Sv32 from the start would give page-level compartment isolation immediately, but it adds real complexity to bring-up at a moment when PMP alone already provides the wall that must exist. We keep the MMU optional and add it deliberately, not reflexively.

Committing to the custom hardware mediation gate now. The v2 vision is an RTL peripheral on the egress bus that enforces capabilities in silicon, unbypassable even by a compromised Monitor (Chapter 13). It is the distinctive contribution, and exactly the sort of thing to attempt only after a software-plus-PMP v1 has taught us what the primitive must do.

4.9Open questions

The honest loose ends specific to the hardware:

  • Whether the Sv32 MMU is enabled for v1 multi-compartment isolation or held for a later revision (shared with Chapter 3).
  • The exact operating frequency the placed-and-routed design sustains, and therefore the final performance envelope.
  • The precise sizes of MON_DATA and SECRETS, pending how much block RAM the fit leaves free.
  • How much of the ESP32 link protocol the Monitor owns directly versus what a user-mode driver formats before the Monitor's gated send.
  • Whether to populate and rely on the SSD1331 OLED in the reference build, or treat it as an optional nicety atop the guaranteed LEDs.
